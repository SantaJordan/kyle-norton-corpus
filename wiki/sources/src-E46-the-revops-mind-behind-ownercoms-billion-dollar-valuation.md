# E46: The RevOps Mind Behind Owner.com's Billion-Dollar Valuation: Steve Dinner
**Show:** The Revenue Leadership Podcast · **Guest:** Steve Dinner, VP RevOps @ Owner.com (transcript: "my VB of RevOps here at owner") · **Published:** 2025-09-10 · **Kyle's role:** host · **Raw:** `raw/podcasts/own-show/E46-the-revops-mind-behind-ownercoms-billion-dollar-valuation/transcript.md`

## TL;DR
- An inside look at how Owner runs RevOps. Dinner keeps "one foot" in revenue leadership: he spends 30–40% of his week in functional rituals and does monthly rep ride-alongs. He solves the underlying business problem rather than the ticket, and accepts 80% solutions so he can win big where the company needs it.
- About a year before this episode, the team moved from ad hoc to agile with help from a consultancy (Aselis). The structure is four product owners (marketing, sales, launch, CS), a project manager, a comp / IT-style help-desk person, and contractors working two-week sprints. Retros feed a RevOps improvement board, and recurring problems become SOPs ("turning variables into constants").
- Kyle's thesis for CROs: take ownership of RevOps, hire for philosophical alignment and clear jobs-to-be-done, and invest earlier than feels necessary. Owner's RevOps team is 7 people at $50M ARR, which he says is cheaper than 100 reps running 2–20% less efficiently.

## What Kyle said
- Origin story: he and Dinner "have worked together for almost a decade". Kyle hired him out of a rep role to be a BDR manager at League "because I interviewed him and he just thought in systems and programmatically". He then became BDR director and took over RevOps, with "stops at Shopify", and has been at Owner "for over two years" with a broad portfolio. @01:00–02:00 ([[person-steve-dinner]], [[sales-hiring]])
- Why he did the episode: he gets "so many questions about infrastructure and tech stack and revop", and he refers RevOps leaders who reach out to Dinner. @01:00–03:00 ([[revops]])
- Framing: good RevOps doesn't solve everything with RevOps. Keep "a light touch with technology", add a validation field only "when it's absolutely required", and add business process only when needed. @08:05 ([[revops]])
- Design philosophy: "this assumption that people want to do well and they're smart and capable". Treat people as untrustworthy, with guardrails and validation fields, and they will act that way. A hundred validation fields create "learned helplessness" and take away agency. @22:23–23:23 ([[culture-and-high-standards]], [[revops]])
- The dashboard trap: "You're dehumanized." @21:22 Standardize the front of a process: "freedom within a framework, baby." @26:27 ([[playbooks-and-documentation]])
- Ride-alongs are "such an important empathy building exercise" that surfaces friction. Use nudge theory to "make our golden path the lowest friction path" and add friction where you don't want people to go. @28:28 ([[sales-coaching-and-enablement]])
- "it's not all tech." In Dinner's ride-along, reps didn't know a follow-up list existed. Good RevOps teams have enough business context to say "this is a coaching opportunity. We don't need to to tech this thing." @31:33 ([[revops]], [[sales-coaching-and-enablement]])
- Hiring a RevOps leader is harder for CROs because "it's not our core skill set". He has managed RevOps for "eight, nine years now". @50:54 Be clear about jobs-to-be-done. Owner's high-velocity model (hundreds of thousands of accounts, thousands of dials a day, hundreds of customers closed a month) makes RevOps about "business strategy, workflow optimization, infrastructure", with analytics as "pure data science" (regression, A/B testing) handled separately. An enterprise RevOps leader could own core reporting. @51:56–52:56 ([[revops]], [[smb-sales-motion]], [[org-design]])
- CROs "have to take more ownership over revops than than they do today". "I don't want to think about reporting" is "a path to pain". As he manages bigger teams and is further from the front line, he sees a pattern: "the importance of like leadership alignment and philosophical alignment". @57:02–58:02 ([[cro-role-and-board]])
- The basketball / NBA 2K analogy: "you only have 100 points to allocate". No single hire has every skill, so pair a RevOps leader with a data partner ("Jonathan"). @58:02–59:03 ([[org-design]], [[sales-hiring]])
- "if you got a good one, keep them close". Example: when Bobby Morrison became Shopify's CRO, the first thing he did was bring in his RevOps leader. @64:10 ([[shopify]])
- The biggest mistake he sees: "invest in this team way earlier than you think you need", or you end up "three or six months behind". Owner hired Dinner full-time at about $5M ARR, started staffing at about $7–8M, and now has seven people (four POs, a PM, comp) and is adding a head of architecture at $50M ARR. "what's expensive is having a hundred frontline go to market reps that are 2% 5% 20% less efficient than they should be." @68:20–69:22 ([[revops]], [[capacity-planning]])
- Function-building means "having a project management framework, agile, like a systematic way to engage with the rest of the business" @74:27. "You're not going to convince anybody to get a bunch of hires unless you have like a pretty solid system". @76:32
- Context from the guest: Kyle asked Dinner to read *Working Backwards* @79:33. Kyle reportedly told him at their first meeting, "We see the world the same way." @55:00

## Guest's core ideas
- Stay a revenue leader. Owning the BDR team and RevOps at once kept him grounded. He spends "30 to 40% of my scheduled week" in functional rituals, mostly with frontline managers. @04:01–06:02 ([[revops]])
- Go one level above the request: "tell me what you're trying to achieve and what happens if it doesn't get done." Don't treat every problem as an automation or validation nail. Ship 80% solutions and be "relentless about winning big in the right places and losing small in the right places". For the past 12 months that has meant focusing on CSM and support over sales. @09:06–13:12 ([[prioritization-5p]])
- People are "inherently reasonable". When you tell them their thing is deprioritized, the hard conversation is part of the job, not an impediment to it. @16:15–17:16 ([[culture-and-high-standards]])
- Rep experience first. The BDR tier-one dashboard showed that visibility alone changes behavior. The further you are from the phone, the more you distrust reps. Reduce cognitive load, batch work, and order it by next best action. @19:21–31:33 ([[sales-coaching-and-enablement]])
- Standardize the repetitive front of a process ("this is the opener that works") so reps have room for their own artistry. Crowdsource the cadence, commit for 30 days of data, then test what reps instinctively wanted to do. @25:26, @34:35–36:37 ([[playbooks-and-documentation]])
- Agile RevOps: write a working contract (sprint length, rituals, definition of done and of ready), run a retro every sprint, and forecast hand-offs to enablement and data engineering. Agile is a maturity model, so start at two or three people, right-size it, and "skip a rung" where possible. @41:43–47:50 ([[revops]], [[org-design]])
- Bezos-style "paper cut" thinking plus Dalio's "another one of those": categorize recurring problems and write one SOP per category. "The whole process is turning variables into constants." @48:51–50:54 ([[playbooks-and-documentation]])
- Advice to CROs: define your business's constraints, because they tell you what leader you need. Owner's constraint is SMB restaurants with "no huge deal that's going to come save the month", so you must "be great everywhere". Show trust rather than just saying it, put RevOps in every room, and give a stack-ranked list of can't-miss company initiatives for yes / no / later decisions. Avoid becoming the "no team" or a ticket-taking "yes team". @53:58–72:24 ([[cro-role-and-board]], [[smb-sales-motion]])
- Leadership lesson: replace emotional intensity with "practical intensity" that is calm, action-based and high-velocity. @76:32–78:33 ([[executive-performance-and-mindset]])
